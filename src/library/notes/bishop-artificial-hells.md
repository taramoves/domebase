## Argument in brief

Bishop surveys participatory art from the Italian Futurist serate to 2011 and refuses the field's default value judgement: that work involving people is automatically more democratic, more ethical and more real than work that makes objects. She calls the post-1989 formation the social turn (her own 2006 coinage) but argues it is more accurately a return to the social, with three moments - 1917, 1968, 1989 - each carrying a utopian re-imagining of art's relation to the social. Her central complaint is methodological: participatory art is explained sociologically (community, agency, empowerment, demonstrable outcomes) and adjudicated ethically (which artist collaborated generously, which exploited collaborators), and the work itself is never examined as art. Historical case studies - Paris Dada, GRAV, the Situationist International, Lebel's Happenings, Tucuman Arde, Boal's Invisible Theatre, Czechoslovak happenings, the Collective Actions Group, the Community Arts Movement, the Artist Placement Group, Culture in Action, Project Unite, delegated performance, pedagogic projects - build the argument that participation has no fixed politics: it served Italian Fascism, Soviet mass spectacle and state socialism as readily as the anti-capitalist left. The conclusion argues that participation and democracy are not interchangeable, that participatory practice now dovetails with neoliberal labour (networks, mobility, project work, affective labour), and that the tension between artistic and social critique should be sustained rather than collapsed. Method is deliberately interdisciplinary, importing political philosophy (Ranciere above all), theatre history and performance studies.

## Key ideas

- **social turn / return to the social** - the post-1989 wave of collaborative, socially embedded art, historicised against two earlier avant-garde moments.
- **participation** - strict definition: people constitute the central artistic medium and material, in the manner of theatre and performance (PDF p.6). Not interactivity, not audience engagement in general.
- **the ethical turn** - judgement of participatory work by the artist's intentions and procedures rather than by the work's artistic identity; a diagnosis she shares with Ranciere's *Malaise dans l'esthetique*.
- **aisthesis / the aesthetic regime** - Ranciere's terms as Bishop adopts them: the aesthetic as an autonomous regime of experience, and the ground of a politics of spectatorship (le partage du sensible).
- **antagonism / consensus** - Bishop's own version of antagonism is thin and occasional: art can operate in a space of antagonism or negation vis-a-vis society. Her consensus/dissensus argument is carried entirely by Ranciere; Laclau does not appear anywhere in the book, and Mouffe appears once only, in an endnote, quoted through Jodi Dean on post-politics (PDF p.366 / printed p.362). There is no agonistic-democratic theory here, and a review that wants Mouffe's agonism must import it.
- **audience as co-producer** - the third term of the social-turn triad: artist becomes collaborator, work becomes project, viewer becomes co-producer. Bishop tracks the historical sequence crowd (1910s) - masses (1920s) - people (1960s/70s) - the excluded (1980s) - community (1990s) - volunteers (2000s).
- **delegated performance** - hiring non-professionals to perform on the artist's behalf; outsourcing authenticity (Ch.8).
- **pedagogic projects** - art converging with education; Beuys, the Free International University and the lecture-action as precedent (Ch.9).
- **artistic vs social critique** - Boltanski and Chiapello's distinction, used in the conclusion to frame the whole field as a clash of incommensurable value systems.
- **documentation problem** - participatory art emphasises process over a definitive image and depends on first-hand experience over long duration, which forecloses critical distance for the critic as much as for the viewer (PDF p.10).

## Where it lands in the review

This is the load-bearing text for the review's negative claim: that participation, immersion and audience activation are not in themselves democratic, ethical or good. It supplies the vocabulary for criticising fulldome work that treats audience involvement as self-validating (the no-failed-works problem), the argument for judging such work as art rather than by its social or ethical yield, and a documented history in which participatory formats served Fascism and state socialism as readily as the left. Chapter 9 sits directly on the review's spectrum: Beuys's lecture-actions and the pedagogic-project genre are the art-historical precedent for the performance lecture and the plain lecture as art formats. What it cannot carry: no fulldome, planetarium, projection or immersive-media case study, no technical account of apparatus, and no agonistic political theory beyond Ranciere.

## Limits

European focus (Bishop states she includes Eastern Europe and South America but not Asia, and only a few US examples). Written 2006-2011: predates the immersive-media boom and post-2015 participation culture; her example of participation's collapse into spectacle is Gormley's One and Other (2009), not platform media. It is a polemic with a moving position - she notes the shift between the 2006 polemic and the 2011 conclusion. No empirical audience research, no data; the judgements are art-critical. Bishop also concedes the visual-analysis problem without solving it: she argues participatory work cannot be grasped from images alone, yet her own evidence is largely documentary.
