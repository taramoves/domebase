Dome as audience-playable audiovisual instrument.

0 Core Question

How can we make it legible to audiences as a surface, space, and audiovisual tool?

How can we give audiences agency to engage with it?

1 Parts

Audience. Takes actions that interact with objects in different ways.

Hub. Keeps track of and routes actions, objects, and contextual elements, drives the output and writes a log.

Dome. Actions, objects, and contextual elements rendered to projection and routed as spatial audio.

Artist. Manages available actions, objects, and contextual elements to create unified experience.

2 Objects

Objects are what the system carries, places and triggers. An object exists whether or not anyone holds it: a person reaches one only through an action the scene allows. Every object carries an id, a shape, an active shape and a sound.

Placement

The dome divides into eight channels, one per sector. The hub tracks every placement: the object’s azimuth and elevation on the dome, and the channel that position falls in.

Azimuth runs clockwise from the front, elevation from the rim to the top of the dome, and the channel is the 45° sector the azimuth falls in.

Playback

A playhead crosses the dome on a recurring loop and starts at the top of the dome. When the playhead’s value equals an object’s value, the object triggers.

Audio Routing

Channels stay fixed: an object operates in the channel of its placement, and the orbit channels map onto the control grid one to one.

3 Calibrate

Until a phone calibrates, it has no cursor: the hub knows what the person does but not where they point.

What the Phone Knows on Its Own

The back of the phone is the pointer and the screen faces the person. The browser reports three angles.

  • Tilt is reliable. The browser measures it against gravity.
  • Heading is not. The browser measures it from whatever direction the phone faced when the page opened (iPhone) or from a compass that steel and electronics pull off (other phones), so every phone is out by its own unknown angle.
  • Seat is unknown. The phone has no idea where in the room it is.

Why the Seat Matters

Someone off-centre who aims at a spot points along a different line from someone at the centre. At distance d from the centre of a dome of radius R, the two directions differ by up to arcsin(d/R) — 30° half-way out, 49° at three-quarters. Calibration supplies the seat, so the hub traces the aim from where the person actually sits.

The Marker

The dome shows five bright dots in an X at the top, about 20° across, with one tip drawn as a ring to mark the front. The person looks up, points the back of the phone at it, and the phone takes the picture. It finds the dots itself and sends their pixel positions, so no image leaves the phone.

The hub knows where each dot sits on the dome, so it solves the phone’s pose from the five dots and gets the seat and the true orientation in one step, then reads the heading offset by comparing that orientation with the phone’s own angles at the same moment.

Browsers do not report focal length. The phone takes a burst of about twenty frames while the hand moves a little: the sensors say how far the phone turned, the dots say how far they moved in the picture, and the difference gives the focal length. It works with whichever rear lens the phone picks.

The Check

The person aims at a mark at the front and taps. A cursor that lands off the mark means calibrating again. The whole exchange takes about ten seconds, and the artist can show the marker in any scene for latecomers or for drift.

4 The Phone

What a Phone Can Do

A phone does five things. The scene decides what the dome makes of each.

actionuses the cursordome result
getnoyour mark becomes the object
aimyescounted by region
tapyesleaves a mark; leaves the object; erases at that spot
hold and releaseyesdraws a line along the path; carries the object; erases along the path
answer a controlnotally bars; moves a racer
sendnoflies off, to a destination or into a collage

Every action gets a screen written for the scene — a press area, a drawing pad, a poll grid, a small game. Underneath, every screen sends the same handful of messages, uses the same cursor and draws from the same set of dome results, so a new screen is a design job and not a new system. A scene that runs its own code on the phone, such as a game, still reports results — a score, a progress, a finish — rather than frames.

Answering and sending need no cursor, so they need no calibration and a latecomer can join them at once. A press reports twice, down and then up, so that a line and a drag both know when the press ended.

The Screen

  • Dark by default. The screen stays black between actions and dims during them, so phones neither light the room nor wash out the projection.
  • One big button. The press area is large enough to hit without looking, with a second button only when the scene needs one, such as send. The phone shows no tool menu; the scene decides what a press means.
  • Awake. The app keeps the screen on, so the phone never locks mid-scene.
  • At the door. Ask people to turn on Do Not Disturb: a web page cannot block notifications.
  • Leaving is logged. The browser reports when the page hides and when it returns.

5 The Scene

A scene sets what the phones show, what the dome does with each action, and what ends it. A show is an ordered list of scenes. The artist writes both; the hub runs them.

A show is a list of scenes with a rule for moving on. Calibration is the first scene and can return at any point, which is how a latecomer joins and how drift is corrected. A phone that joins mid-scene receives the current prompt; if the scene needs a cursor and the phone is not calibrated, the artist offers the marker instead.

show “study no. 1”
  1  calibrate        marker, then the front-mark check   advance when 80% are calibrated
  2  spatial poll     aim → counted by region           advance after 60 s
  3  fridge magnets   the scene above                     advance on the artist
  4  send drawings    draw → send → flies off         advance on the artist
  5  tap race         answer → moves a racer            advance on a winner

The artist works from four controls: next, back, show the marker, blackout. The console is another client of the hub, so it runs on a laptop or a phone, or takes cues from the visuals software over OSC. Scenes advance on a share of the audience rather than all of it, since some phones are always idle, and every scene still has to read as an image if nobody takes part.

6 The Hub

The hub keeps a state for every phone, runs the scene the artist chose, drives the output and logs everything.

hellophone id, screen size

objectkind and content, or a file upload

hidden, visiblethe phone left or returned to the app

Phones send raw angles and the hub applies each phone’s own calibration. Aim is a stream, so a lost message does not matter; everything else is an event, arrives once, and the hub acknowledges it and ignores a repeat.

The hub sends OSC to the visuals and the spatial audio: cursor positions, objects appearing, moving, placed or sent, polls, scores. It emits at a fixed 30 Hz whatever the phone count, and positions travel as azimuth and elevation for the renderer to project. Playheads run on the hub clock, so the ring that is drawn and the notes it triggers come from one tick and cannot drift apart.

Every message goes into one line of log with the hub’s time, the phone id, the scene and the phone’s seat; aim is thinned to five per second and ids carry nothing personal. The log answers four questions: who joined and how long calibration took, how accurate the calibration and the presses were, who took part in each scene and from where in the room, and when phones left the app.

7 Build

Six phases, each ending in something testable.

  1. Network and shell. A phone joins by QR over HTTPS, is granted motion and camera, and holds a WebSocket to a hub that acknowledges and logs everything it sends.
  2. Calibration. A phone solves its seat and its heading from the marker, and the front-mark check confirms both.
  3. Cursor. A calibrated phone moves its mark on the dome from any seat, at a fixed rate.
  4. Interactions. Every action and dome result becomes a rule a scene can call, with a sound of its own and a playhead that reads it.
  5. Scene system. Scenes and shows written as files and run end to end under the artist’s console.
  6. Log and evaluation. One log file per show and an analysis that answers the four questions in the hub section.
